## What the actual bonus chapter reportedly covers (secondhand — not independently verified)
Per third-party summaries that have read the downloaded chapter[^1], it reportedly covers:

- **Core philosophy**: tagging should serve action, not abstract classification — Forte's advice is to spend energy thinking about *how a note will be used*, not what category it technically belongs to (a direct extension of PARA's actionability principle, already covered in [[Chapter 5 - Organize - Save for Actionability]], to a cross-cutting layer on top of folders).
- **When to start**: explicitly wait until you have hundreds of notes and have completed several projects before investing in tagging — tagging too early is framed as "tagging for the sake of tagging," adding overhead with no real payoff yet (this matches the caution already noted in [[Chapter 7 - Express - Show Your Work]]'s footnotes).
- **Tags should describe context, not content** — not what a note is *about* (that's what folders/PARA and the note's own text are for), but the situational role it plays: by final product it will feed into (Presentation, Essay, Report, Website, Project plan, Meeting agenda, Budget); by kind of content (Argument, Theory, Framework, Evidence, Claim, Counterpoint, Question); by project role (Meeting notes, Timeline, Budget, Decision, Action, Idea, Objective); or by workflow stage (Planned, In process, Waiting for approval, Reviewed, Approved, On hold, Finished).
- Tags remain explicitly secondary to PARA — a flexible, optional layer for cross-cutting retrieval (per Chapter 7's third retrieval method), never the primary organizing system.

## Why this (lack of a) chapter is still worth a note
Even without the full content, there's a genuinely interesting, honest observation here: the book's main text treats tagging as optional enough that it doesn't even include the material directly — it's gated behind a separate opt-in. That's consistent with (and arguably stronger evidence for) the caution already flagged in earlier chapters: Forte considers tagging advanced enough, and risky enough to over-adopt prematurely, that he keeps it structurally separate from the core CODE/PARA system a reader encounters by default.

## First-principles breakdown
**The claim, stripped of branding:** cross-cutting situational tags (by output format, content type, project role, or workflow stage) solve a specific retrieval problem that folders can't — finding "everything currently waiting for my manager's approval" or "every unused statistic I've saved" cuts across PARA's project/area/resource boundaries entirely, which is exactly the gap Chapter 7 already identified tags as filling.

**What has to be true for that to hold:** this only pays off if the cross-cutting need is real and recurring (not a one-off), since tagging consistently enough to make it searchable has a real, ongoing cost — which is precisely why Forte's own advice (per the secondhand summaries) is to delay tagging until you have enough volume and project history to know which cross-cutting categories you'll actually reuse, rather than guessing upfront. This is consistent with — not a new idea beyond — the general principle running through this whole book: add structure only once a real, observed need justifies its ongoing maintenance cost (the same logic behind "don't build Areas/Resources folders before you need them" in Chapter 5, and "distill only with a purpose in mind" in Chapter 6).

## Deeper commentary — how this applies to your setup specifically
This vault doesn't need a tagging layer yet, per this chapter's own explicit advice (and the same conclusion already reached in [[Chapter 7 - Express - Show Your Work]]) — folders (PARA) and Obsidian's native search are handling retrieval adequately so far. If a genuine cross-cutting need arises later (for example, wanting to find every book-note chapter across every book that touches "retrieval" or "first-principles" as a theme), a lightweight tag convention at that point would be the right trigger — not before.
